Does the definition of success change who succeeds?

Two leagues of 64 basketball teams, identical at season zero, playing the same game under the same physics. The only difference between them is what counts as a win: League A rewards any victory, League B only rewards winning by a set margin. Then both evolve for hundreds of seasons — the teams that score well have more offspring — and we watch what each league becomes.

Everything below is simulated live in your browser. Turn the knobs and watch: every control except the seed applies mid-run, so you can raise the bar at season 300 and see the population turn from where it stands.

What you are looking at
  • Evolution finishes early. Capability climbs from roughly zero to its ceiling and is ~75% of the way there by season 100, ~95% by season 200, and done by about season 300. Everything after that is drift around an equilibrium: the attribute budget is fully spent and offense and defense sit near their caps, so there is nothing left to improve. Running thousands of seasons adds noise, not information — 600 is already twice what the model needs.
  • Both channels do real work. Knock out within-lifetime strategy learning (drop both mutation sliders to their minimum) and final capability falls from ~77 to ~34. Knock out attribute mutation instead and it settles near ~43. Weaken selection (replaced each season → 0.05) and the climb takes three times as long. None of this is drift.
  • The match engine is reward-blind — it never sees a threshold. Anything that diverges does so through selection, not through a rule written into the physics.
  • Both leagues start from an identical population each restart, so any gap you see opened up during the run.
  • 50% on the win-rate chart is not "the same". It means neither population wins more single games on fresh legs — which is the least informative thing about them. They still differ in the shape of their results (B wins bigger and loses bigger), in tournaments (A takes ~57% of titles, because six straight wins compounds B’s fatigue), and the measure is zero-sum, so it would read 50% even if both populations had got worse together.
  • Which number to watch? Two, because they answer different questions. Real capability is absolute — expected points against a fixed neutral team, counting both offense and defense — so it tells you whether a population is getting better at the game at all. B’s win rate against A is relative and zero-sum, so it tells you who is ahead but cannot distinguish “both improved” from “both declined”. Neither sees fatigue: both are fresh-legs measures, and League B’s real weakness shows up most in deep tournament runs.
  • Past about 25, the league stops working — but not in the obvious way. The aggression gap does not collapse; it plateaus around 0.18–0.22 all the way to 50. What breaks is skill maintenance. Real capability holds steady up to a bar of ~20 and then falls off a cliff: at 50, League B’s offense decays from 0.93 to 0.72, drifting back toward the 0.5 it started from, because so few games score that selection can no longer tell good teams from bad. Margin volatility triples, but that is the population fragmenting rather than any team playing riskier — the spread of aggression across teams doubles too. Set the bar to 40 or 50 and watch capability sag while volatility explodes.
  • Price of volatility is the parameter that decides whether a high bar degrades real skill or merely adds noise. Set it low and watch League B trade capability away; set it high and the skill gap closes while the volatility gap survives.
  • One run cannot show the capability result. The converged gap is about −0.5 points per 100 possessions, and single runs scatter from −2.3 to +0.4 — so roughly one run in four shows League B ahead. Raise Replicates to 8 or more and the shaded band tells you whether a gap is real. At 1 replicate there is no band, and there should not be.
