Change the definition of winning, and you change who wins.

Two leagues of 64 teams start from byte-identical populations and play the same game under the same physics. One difference: what counts as a success. After 600 seasons of selection, watch what each league became.

What 600 seasons of selection did

Population averages across 16 independent replicates, shaded with 95% confidence intervals. Both leagues began at the same point on every line.

The divergence, measured

Each row is the mean over the last 30 seasons, paired by replicate — both leagues share a starting population within a seed, so the per-seed difference removes starting luck entirely. But a confidence interval excluding zero only says the two leagues ended up different — identically rewarded populations drift apart too. The last column is the one that matters: it tests each effect against a null control where both leagues run the same rule. Two effects that look significant fail that test.

So which league is better?

A league can only grade itself on its own scale, so the reward rates above are not comparable. The fair test is a neutral bracket: 32 evolved teams from each league, plain win/loss, no threshold, no further learning.

Better at what, exactly?

Here is the trap: every yardstick you could pick is one league’s home rule. Judge by win rate and A wins by construction; judge by blowouts and B does. So don’t pick a bar — sweep every bar. Both populations play the same opponent, and we ask what share of games clear a bar of k, for every k.

Wins games, loses tournaments

A championship is six straight wins. League B evolved high aggression and spent its attribute budget on risk capacity rather than stamina — and aggression amplifies fatigue. Both sides are equally rested at every point below; the slope is what a deep run costs B.

Put them in one league and let them fight

Thirty-two teams from each upbringing, one combined league, left to compete. Lineage is traced through reproduction, so this measures ancestry rather than this season’s form. One ancestry usually takes the whole league, so the meaningful number is how often. Under neutral drift it would be 50%.

The third row is the interesting one: a bar redrawn at random every season, which no team can see coming. Note what decides it — not the uncertainty, but the average height of the bars in the draw. Sampling uniformly from 1 to 10 means nine of ten worlds have a high bar, and being built for high bars wins there even while being worse at the underlying game. Narrow that range toward 1 and the result would flip.

What this does and doesn’t show

  • The match engine is reward-blind — a test asserts its code never references a threshold, a reward, or a league. Every difference you see is selection acting on strategy, not a rule written into the physics.
  • Both leagues start from byte-identical populations per replicate, so starting-population luck cannot explain the gap.
  • How cheaply a team can buy volatility is a tunable of the engine, so the magnitude here is not a physical constant. The direction is robust, and it scales the way you would expect: raising League B’s bar from 5 points to 10 roughly tripled the gap in aggression and widened every downstream effect.
  • Teams have a fixed attribute budget, so evolution must trade one capacity against another rather than improving at everything at once.
